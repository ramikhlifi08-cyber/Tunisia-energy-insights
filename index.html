Understanding Tunisia's Electricity Challenges Through Research and Data

Tunisia Energy Insights researches and analyzes electricity outage patterns using verified public sources, and shares information about energy reliability and possible solutions.

Tunisia faces increasing pressure on its electricity system, especially during periods of high demand. Power outages can affect homes, businesses, education, healthcare, communication, and daily life. This project researches those patterns using verified public sources and turns them into understandable, cited data.

ABOUT THE PROJECT

Why I started this project

I created Tunisia Energy Insights because of my connection to Tunisia and my interest in using technology and data to understand real-world problems. Electricity reliability affects daily life, education, businesses, healthcare, and economic development.

My goal is not to claim that I can solve Tunisia's entire energy challenge. Instead, I want to research and document what's happening using verified public sources, make that information easier to understand, and encourage informed discussion about possible solutions.

Project goals

  • Research Tunisia's electricity reliability challenges using verified public sources
  • Identify patterns in outage timing, causes, and regions
  • Create understandable, clearly cited summaries
  • Educate visitors about energy reliability
  • Explore renewable energy and conservation solutions
  • Publish transparent, source-linked findings

Methodology

This project synthesizes information from published news reporting, utility announcements, and institutional sources such as the World Bank and international energy trackers. Every figure is linked to its original source. Reporting can be incomplete or change over time, so findings should be read as a snapshot based on the sources cited — not as this project's own independently verified statistics.

Project founder

Rami Khlifi

Rami Khlifi is a Tunisian-American high school student and the founder of Tunisia Energy Insights. Raised between the United States and Tunisia, he has watched electricity outages disrupt everyday life for family and friends back home — interrupted schoolwork, spoiled food, and businesses losing hours of operation. That firsthand connection is what drove him to start this project. Rami is especially interested in Information Systems, data analysis, and how technology can make complex, real-world problems easier to understand. He built Tunisia Energy Insights not to claim he can fix Tunisia's electricity grid, but to give residents a way to document what they're experiencing and to make that information visible, structured, and useful to researchers, journalists, and policymakers.

VERIFIED DATA · 2025–2026

Tunisia's electricity system, by the numbers

Figures below are drawn from published reporting and institutional sources, not from this project's own (not-yet-collected) community data. Each figure links to its source.

Demand has outpaced supply

In July 2026, national electricity demand surged roughly 30% during a severe heatwave to around 5,000 MW, exceeding STEG's generation capacity of about 4,630 MW and forcing rotating cuts.

Source: African Manager, 2026 →

A record peak

STEG's director general reported national consumption reached a record 6,000 megawatts during the July 2026 heatwave, which officials attributed largely to intensive air-conditioning use.

Source: Euronews, July 2026 →

Demand keeps climbing

Government projections show annual electricity demand growing 2.5–3.5% per year from 2026–2030, requiring up to 600 additional megawatts of generating capacity.

Source: Middle East Online, July 2026 →

Heavy reliance on gas

Tunisia's generation system is dominated by gas-fired plants. At end-2024, installed capacity stood at 6.9 GW: about 75% gas, 11% solar, 9% oil, 3.5% wind, and 1% hydro. Roughly three-quarters of the gas Tunisia consumes is imported from or transited via Algeria.

Source: Enerdata →

Abundant, underused sunshine

Tunisia receives roughly 3,000 hours of sunshine per year — about twice as many as Germany — yet solar still supplied only about 11% of installed generation capacity as of late 2024.

Source: Blackout News, August 2026 →

A renewables target — revised upward

Tunisia has raised its official target for renewables' share of the electricity mix to 35% by 2030 (up from an earlier 30% goal), with a longer-term goal of 50% by 2035.

Source: Ecofin Agency, 2026 →

A financially strained utility

STEG's total debt reached 7.36 billion dinars by June 2026, while unpaid bills owed to the company — by both private customers and public institutions — reached 6.06 billion dinars.

Source: Nawaat, July 2026 →

Not the first time

Tunisia used similar rotating cuts during the heatwaves of 2023 and 2025, and experienced a near-total nationwide blackout in September 2023 following a breakdown at the Radès power station south of Tunis.

Source: Carthage Magazine, July 2026 →

International investment underway

In November 2025, the World Bank approved a $430 million program (TEREG) to help Tunisia add 2.8 GW of new solar and wind capacity by 2028, cut electricity supply costs by 23%, and raise STEG's cost recovery from 60% to 80%.

Source: World Bank, November 2025 →

Demand vs. capacity

Electricity demand can exceed available generation capacity during peak periods, especially in extreme heat, forcing utilities to manage load carefully.

Heat and air conditioning

Widespread air-conditioning use during hot months sharply increases peak demand, often concentrated in a few hours each afternoon and evening.

Generation, transmission, distribution

Generation creates electricity, transmission moves it over long distances at high voltage, and distribution delivers it to homes and businesses.

Planned vs. unplanned outages

Planned outages are scheduled for maintenance or load management; unplanned outages result from faults, overloads, or unexpected failures.

Energy conservation

Reducing consumption during peak hours can ease pressure on the grid and lower the likelihood of load-shedding.

Solar energy

Tunisia has strong solar potential. Distributed solar generation can supplement grid supply, particularly during high-demand daylight hours.

Battery storage

Storage technology allows excess energy generated at one time to be used later, helping smooth out supply and demand mismatches.

Energy efficiency

Efficient appliances, lighting, and insulation reduce overall electricity use without reducing comfort or productivity.

Smart-grid technology

Smart grids use sensors and real-time data to detect problems faster and balance load more precisely across the network.

What households can do

  • Reduce unnecessary electricity use during peak-demand periods
  • Use energy-efficient lighting and appliances
  • Maintain air-conditioning systems
  • Avoid running several high-energy appliances simultaneously
  • Improve home insulation where possible
  • Consider solar energy only after reviewing cost, safety, maintenance, and local regulations
  • Keep flashlights, charged power banks, and emergency supplies available

Desk research summary

July–August 2026 Outage Overview

Published August 20, 2026

Tunisia saw a sustained wave of rotating power cuts through July and August 2026, driven by extreme heat. STEG reported national demand hit a record 6,000 MW in late July — about 30% above typical levels — against installed capacity of roughly 4,630 MW. Cuts were generally short and rotating, often 30–45 minutes in the hardest-hit neighborhoods, aimed at avoiding a full grid collapse rather than punishing any one area. Not every outage was heat-related: STEG also ran planned maintenance during this window, including line reinforcement in Sfax and testing ahead of a new transformer station in Kairouan.

Desk research summary

Regional Comparison: Where Cuts Were Concentrated

Published August 20, 2026

Published STEG cut schedules show the south bearing a disproportionate share of load-shedding: an early-August 2026 notice named Gabès, Djerba, Medenine, Zarzis, Ben Guerdane, Tataouine, Kébili, Gafsa, Tozeur, and Sidi Bouzid. A separate mid-July notice covered Greater Tunis, the North, the Centre, and Sfax on a rotating schedule. Ground-level reporting from La Soukra and Tunis's southern suburbs described outages concentrated in the hottest afternoon hours. This is a qualitative summary of named regions in public announcements, not a quantified ranking — that requires this project's own report volume by governorate.

Sources: 3 outletsBasis: STEG cut notices

Blackout News · Capmad · Carthage Magazine

Desk research summary

Summer 2026 Reliability Under Extreme Heat

Published August 20, 2026

Temperatures topped 45°C in several regions during summer 2026, with Mateur (Bizerte governorate) reaching 47.5°C — 8 to 14°C above seasonal norms, per the National Meteorological Institute. STEG's chief executive warned that without active load management the country risked a complete outage, and said the utility was coordinating with Algeria and Libya to strengthen cross-border interconnections. Government projections call for up to 600 additional MW of capacity by 2030 to keep pace with 2.5–3.5% annual demand growth.

Desk research summary

Renewable Energy & Solutions: Where Tunisia Stands

Published August 20, 2026

Tunisia raised its renewables target to 35% of the electricity mix by 2030 (up from 30%) and 50% by 2035. As of 2025, installed solar PV capacity reached roughly 900 MW — about three-quarters of total renewable capacity, which stood near 1.2 GW, still a small slice of the country's 6.9 GW installed base (~75% gas-fired). Recent additions include a 120 MW plant brought online by Amea Power in December 2025, 50 MW plants in Tozeur and Mezouna (Sidi Bouzid), and an operating plant in Metbasta, Kairouan, part of a national push for 500 MW of solar across five governorates. In March 2026, authorities tendered a 300 MW solar park paired with 150 MW/540 MWh of battery storage, and the World Bank approved a $430 million program in November 2025 to add 2.8 GW of new solar and wind by 2028.
